Key Fundamentals of Conflict Resolution (vs. The Letter’s Approach)

  • Reciprocity: Authentic peace requires mutual respect and a baseline of dignity. Instead, the author employs dehumanizing mockery, personal attacks, and ad hominem insults.

  • Neutrality: A successful process involves neutral mediation to find common ground. The author rejects this, positioning himself as the sole arbiter of “history” and “justice”.

  • Shared Responsibility: Sustainable peace acknowledges that conflict involves multiple actors. The author uses the term “Your war” throughout to absolve his side of any role in the conflict’s origin or perpetuation.

  • Collaborative Framing: Instead of identifying shared goals, the author issues ultimatums and demands total compliance.

  • Integrity of Process: Effective resolution relies on established diplomatic protocols like “formulas” and “shuttle diplomacy”. The author explicitly rejects these as “time lost,” favoring a process he can personally control.

Why This is the Diametric Opposite of a Peace Process

  • The Rejection of Agency: A true peace process acknowledges that both parties have legitimate concerns that must be addressed to reach a stable resolution. By framing the adversary solely as a failing “dictator” who “forces” his people, the author denies the existence of any legitimate Russian stakeholder concern.

  • The Weaponization of Data: Rather than using data for transparency or understanding, the author weaponizes casualty statistics to project dominance and demand surrender—a tactic intended to break the adversary’s will rather than reconcile differences.

  • The Subversion Objective: By explicitly stating he will “work toward” the fatigue of the adversary and foster domestic unrest, the author declares that his goal is the internal destabilization of the Russian state. This is the functional definition of a continuation of the war, not an exit strategy.

  • Ultimatum vs. Negotiation: The document concludes not by inviting a discussion, but by setting a binary take it or leave it ultimatum. A peace process seeks common ground; an ultimatum seeks to impose one’s own will on the other.
