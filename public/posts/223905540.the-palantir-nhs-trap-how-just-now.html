The Palantir NHS Trap: How ‘Just Now’ Becomes Forever

Published on 7 November 2026 · https://iq2qq.com/p/the-palantir-nhs-trap-how-just-now

The UK government had a choice. A break point in the controversial £330 million Federated Data Platform (FDP) contract gave health ministers a clean exit ramp. It could have declined the first extension, ended the deal with US firm Palantir, and begun reclaiming the digital spine of the National Health Service.

Instead, it blinked.

Citing the risk of immediate operational disruption and the need to protect the waiting list, the Department of Health and Social Care is preparing a two-year extension. The underlying rationale is that the system has been allowed to become essentially too integrated to fail, with an internal review concluding it would be "too disruptive" to replace the vendor immediately. Ministers present the 24-month buffer as a pragmatic, temporary step while they prepare a “sovereign” British alternative.

Anyone familiar with how large technology platforms entrench themselves knows this logic is self-fulfilling. Keeping Palantir embedded ‘just for now’ makes exit progressively harder.

This is not a new development. It is the logical next stage of the process we documented in The Bio-Digital Heist and The 200-Dose Indictment. What was previously described as an emerging inverted, top-down utility controlled by corporate and globalist networks is now being locked in under the language of operational necessity.

Absurdity of Vendor Lock-In

By declining to sever the relationship at the first available point, the government has advanced a systemic entrenchment. Over the next two years Palantir’s software will be woven further into the workflows, databases, and administrative habits of the 142 NHS trusts currently described as live on the platform.

When the next decision point arrives, the same argument will be recycled: extraction is now even more disruptive because the system is more entangled. What makes the decision particularly striking is that the contract itself contains standard termination and assistance provisions designed to manage an orderly exit. Campaign groups have pointed out that these include bridging support obligations of up to 24 months that undercut claims of sudden chaos. Ministers had a contractual pathway to end the arrangement without an abrupt blackout. They chose not to use it.

This is the same lock-in dynamic we warned about when examining how local data, school registries, and GP contracts were being fed into a centralised machine. The Federated Data Platform is simply the software engine that makes that system operational at national scale.

The Revolving Door

To treat this solely as political naivety understates the institutional pattern. Former Labour deputy leader, lord Watson—once Shadow Digital Secretary—joined Palantir as Senior Vice President for the UK in September 2026. He had already been a paid adviser to the company since November 2024 (sitting on its Public Services Advisory Board) and a paid adviser to the lobby firm Lodestone, which represented Palantir as a client. Reporting shows he wrote to Health Secretary Yvette Cooper in the weeks before the extension decision, arguing that continuing the relationship would strengthen Britain’s tech position.

The systemic nature of this capture becomes undeniable when looking at Lodestone's deeper tentacles; as revealed, Lodestone's co-founder, Martha Dalton, explicitly stepped away from lobbying for Palantir to run strategic communications and campaign rebuttals for Andy Burnham, embedded directly in the team that paved his return to parliament and his eventual entry into Downing Street.

In earlier reporting we detailed how Peter Mandelson facilitated alignment with Palantir — his firm Global Counsel represented the company until late 2025, and he took Keir Starmer on an unminuted visit to Palantir’s headquarters. Under Wes Streeting the pressure continued: the then Health Secretary publicly criticised the “glacial” pace of FDP adoption, told trusts to “go further, faster,” and oversaw guidance that pushed wider uptake even as some hospitals reported the system was slower or less useful than tools they already had. The actors have changed under Andy Burnham, but the network and the agenda remain unbroken. When senior political figures move directly into corporate executive roles or maintain close advisory ties, the boundary between public service and private interest becomes harder to maintain. Party labels matter less than the continuity of access.

This seamless transition from public office to corporate payroll is explicitly mirrored in state security. An investigation by The Bureau of Investigative Journalism revealed that former Metropolitan Police Commissioner Dame Cressida Dick has been working as a paid adviser to Palantir since September 2025.

Her appointment underscores a broader, cross-departmental capture; it coincides with Palantir securing a new £10 million contract with Bedfordshire, Cambridgeshire, and Hertfordshire police forces to deploy its Foundry platform under "Project Nectar"—advancing alongside an existing £9 million contract running the national firearms licensing system for all UK police forces. Alongside political figures like Tom Watson, Palantir has systematically absorbed high-ranking defense officials, including former Conservative defence minister Leo Docherty and former Ministry of Defence second permanent secretary Laurence Lee, effectively turning public service credibility into a commercial asset.

Broader alignments reinforce the picture. NHS England participates in World Economic Forum networks in which Palantir also holds strategic partner status — the exact transnational architecture flagged in previous analysis of the Tony Blair Institute and Davos influence over UK health policy. The opacity around some of these connections reinforce public suspicion that long-term institutional relationships are being managed out of view.

Resistance on the Ground

While Whitehall prepares the extension, resistance is visible at trust level. Freedom of Information responses show that 29 of the 137 trusts NHS England lists as “realising benefits” state that the platform is not live or that they are not using its applications. Earlier FOI analysis found roughly a third of signed-up trusts had recorded no meaningful activity on key apps over a year. Local boards protective of patient data are quietly declining full deployment.

This is the practical expression of the resistance we called for previously. While Whitehall and corporate marketing teams tout the platform as a triumph of operational efficiency—promising streamlined surgery scheduling and faster hospital discharges—society is increasingly treating the issue as one of control rather than mere software choice. A US company with significant defence and intelligence contracts managing core NHS operational data, combined with unelected international forums, has made the Federated Data Platform a political flashpoint.

Power of the Opt-Out

If central government will not prioritise data sovereignty, patients retain direct tools. The National Data Opt-Out and Type 1 opt-outs at GP level allow individuals to withhold consent for secondary uses of their data. Widespread use of these mechanisms reduces the volume of data flowing into centralised platforms. The earlier calls to reclaim biological and data sovereignty were not abstract. They remain the most immediate lever available.

The NHS belongs to the public. Allowing health data to become permanently locked behind a corporate gate under the banner of temporary convenience removes the practical ability to reverse the decision later.

The two-year extension buys time for corporate consolidation—the infrastructure is no longer a theoretical threat; it is a contractual reality tightening around the health service, and this window is the public’s penultimate practical opportunity to pull the plug.
