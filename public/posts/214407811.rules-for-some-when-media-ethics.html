Rules for Some: When Media ‘Ethics’ on Appearance Depend on the Target

Mainstream outlets maintain elaborate public standards against body-shaming and hair discrimination. They treat appearance-based mockery as toxic, dehumanising, and, in the case of natural hair texture, a form of racial injustice that can cost people jobs and damage mental health. The Independent has an entire topic archive devoted to “body shaming”. It has published years of advocacy framing Afro hair discrimination as a civil-rights issue requiring legal protection, school-policy reform, and corporate codes of conduct.

Those standards are real. They are simply not applied evenly.

The pattern in practice

In August and September 2026, the same outlet produced a cluster of features on one political figure’s hair:

  • 7 August: “‘Aging backwards’ or wearing a weave?” The piece opens by noting that the hairstyle “has set the internet ablaze with chatter, as fans praise and critics mock his appearance.” It catalogues claims of a weave, comparisons of earlier thinner hair by the official Democratic Party account, and declarations that “Trump has a weave!!”

  • 3 September: A follow-up examines bald-spot sightings, thinning hair, and an elaborate description of “an absolutely clean pate – a contained island after scalp reduction surgery” maintained by dye and stiffening spray. The subject’s attempt to dispel the rumours is undercut with photographic counter-evidence.

  • 6 September: “‘What’s going on with Trump’s hair?’” Photos of darker colour and a wind-blown scalp are paired with the same partisan mockery. One quoted line compares the look to “serial killer Rex Heuermann.” The paper does not merely reprint the joke; it hyperlinks the name directly to its own detailed true-crime feature on the Gilgo Beach murders — a piece that recounts the strangling and dismemberment of multiple women.

Across these articles the outlet acknowledges that critics are mocking appearance, then elevates the mockery, frames age-related changes as newsworthy, and in one case engineers a direct pathway from hair colour to coverage of a mass murderer.

The contrasting standard

Weeks and months around the same period, The Independent treated appearance-based attacks differently when the direction of fire was reversed:

  • The DeLauro/Cheung Clash: An exchange between Trump aide Steven Cheung and Democratic Representative Rosa DeLauro was explicitly headlined as a “body-shame brawl” after Cheung targeted the lawmaker’s appearance (”Blue-haired Democrat freak”) and a previous White House account post mocked her hair (”Even worse hair”).

  • Laura Loomer & Pete Hegseth: Loomer’s comments on Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s weight were framed as “body shaming,” with the backlash presented as justified. Similarly, Hegseth’s social-media post about a Canadian teenage cadet triggered “bipartisan backlash” and explicit accusations of body-shaming a teenager, treated as a serious ethical failing.

This double standard persists completely unchecked even when contrasted against the administration’s own appeals. As The Independent has elsewhere noted, White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt explicitly urged political opponents and the media to tone down hostile language and stop the “systemic demonization” of the president and his supporters. Yet the outlet routinely brushes past those warnings—archiving them as mere political talking points while simultaneously choosing to curate, host, and amplify appearance-based ridicule.

A review of the outlet’s body-shaming coverage shows the imbalance is not accidental. Strong moral condemnation of “body shaming” is far more consistently applied when the attacker is Republican or MAGA-aligned and the target is progressive or Democratic. Parallel cases involving Democrats receive softer, or primarily political-framing treatment.

The defences do not hold

  • “Just reporting the discourse” fails once editors select the most extreme insults, reprint them across multiple pieces, acknowledge the mockery, and hyperlink the worst comparison to a graphic true-crime story. That is curation and amplification.

  • “Public figures are fair game” collapses against the outlet’s own record. It does not treat body-shaming of AOC, a teenager, or Representative DeLauro as fair game; it condemns it.

  • “He has mocked others” converts a claimed universal ethical standard into a transactional one. If appearance-based ridicule is wrong when directed at approved targets, the principle cannot become optional when the target is disliked.

Why it matters

This is not about any single politician’s hair. It is about whether stated media ethics are principles or preferences. When an outlet maintains parallel moral universes — one in which hair discrimination and body-shaming are urgent equality issues, and another in which sustained appearance-based ridicule of a political rival is ordinary content — the standards themselves are exposed as conditional.

The rules exist. They are simply switched on or off depending on the target. Documenting that switch, using the outlet’s own headlines, topic pages, acknowledgements, and internal hyperlinks, is the point of an MSM Watch file.
