The Secret Disservice: Inside MI5's License to Lie—and the Highest Crimes

Published on 19 November 2026 · https://iq2qq.com/p/the-secret-disservice-inside-mi5s

On November 3, 2026, three of the most senior judges in England and Wales—lady chief justice baroness Carr, dame Victoria Sharp, and Mr justice Chamberlain—handed down a ruling that turned the British ‘Rule of Law’ into a bad joke.

In hm attorney general v BBC, MI5 had already formally admitted, without reservation, that it gave false evidence based on lies to three separate courts. Those lies were told to conceal the truth about “Agent X”—a violent neo-Nazi informant, known misogynist, and serial domestic abuser whom MI5 shielded from accountability and helped relocate abroad while under police investigation for coercively controlling his partner and attacking her with a machete. An independent investigation led by sir John Goldring confirmed “serious and systemic failings” inside the Security Service.

If an ordinary British citizen lies to a judge, they face perjury or contempt of court. Yet when MI5 stood exposed as having repeatedly misled the courts, the high court ruled that holding the agency or its officers in contempt was “not in the public interest.”

Within eleven days of that ruling, the rest of the picture blew wide open. On November 13, the former detective who led the 1998 Omagh bombing investigation testified under oath that the man who transported the car bomb that killed 29 people—including a woman pregnant with twins—was a Gardaí agent with a “high potential” of having been jointly handled by UK security services, accusing intelligence agencies of obstructing his investigation. One day later, on November 14, leaked internal emails published by The Grayzone revealed that MI5 director general sir Ken McCallum and former chief baroness Eliza Manningham-Buller had been quietly lobbying allies in the House of lords to weaken or kill the Hillsborough Law—the exact legislation designed to make lying to public inquiries a criminal offence carrying up to 14 years in prison.

Put those pieces together, and you aren’t looking at an isolated “administrative error.” You are looking at an unaccountable intelligence apparatus operating above the law—protected by judges who refuse to punish courtroom deception under existing rules, and backed by spy chiefs working behind closed doors to stop parliament from passing a new one.

Act I: Three Judicial Excuses for a Whitewash

To be fair to the bench, the high court faced genuine procedural constraints—re-litigating classified intelligence files in open contempt proceedings is legally complex, and judges are understandably wary of compromising sensitive operational methods. Yet to appreciate the sheer pageantry of the November 3 ruling, you only need to read the judges’ own words. baroness Carr, dame Victoria Sharp, and Mr justice Chamberlain acknowledged that MI5’s conduct fell “badly short.” Then they rolled out a three-step legal carpet to let the agency walk away unscathed:

  1. Too Important to Fine. The judges ruled against holding MI5 liable as an institution because “any sanction available against MI5 could be financial only. There could be no public interest in imposing a financial penalty whose only effect would be to deprive MI5 of funds it requires to undertake its important work.” Imagine trying that defence at a magistrates’ court: Your Honour, fining me would deprive me of funds I require to undertake my important work. As DAT Green pointed out in Prospect magazine, water companies and charities are routinely fined even when that money could otherwise be spent on public services—yet for MI5, perjury comes with a taxpayer-funded shield.

  2. The “Directing Mind” Escape Hatch. Even though senior officers (”Officer 2,” “Officer 3,” and Deputy Director “Witness A”) gave false evidence across three courts, the high court concluded they did not represent MI5’s “directing mind and will.” In other words, when MI5 succeeds, the institution takes the credit; when MI5 lies to judges, nobody at the top was technically driving the car.

  3. Too Much Paperwork for the Court. What about punishing the individual officers who told the lies? The judges noted that sir John Goldring’s report was inadmissible in contempt proceedings against individuals who disputed it, meaning the court would have to re-litigate 36 secret witnesses and classified files. That, the bench declared, would be “a highly resource-intensive matter” it lacked the personnel to handle—passing the buck to the police and crown Prosecution Service (cPS) instead.

This creates a convenient Catch-22. Once three of the highest judges in the land declare there is “no public interest” in initiating contempt proceedings, what are the chances the cPS—applying its own strict public interest test—suddenly decides to prosecute those same officers for misconduct in public office?

Act II: A Pattern of Systemic Obstruction—From Omagh to Manchester

If Agent X were a one-off, MI5’s promises of internal reform might carry a shred of credibility. Instead, the public record over the past year reads like a rap sheet of institutional obstruction:

  • The Omagh Bombing Inquiry (November 2026): Giving evidence in Belfast, retired Detective Chief Superintendent Norman Baxter—who led the investigation into the August 1998 Real IRA bombing that murdered 29 people—testified that the man who transported the bomb vehicle was a covert agent for the Gardaí with a “high potential” of having been jointly handled by MI5. Baxter stated that Special Branch, MI5, and GCHQ knew the identities of suspects beforehand, allowed the cell significant freedom of action despite clear opportunities to intervene, and obstructed his investigation. As bereaved father Michael Gallagher noted outside the hearing, without a statutory public inquiry, Baxter would have been forced by official secrecy laws to take that truth to his grave.

  • Operation Kenova & Stakeknife (December 2025 – February 2026): A nine-year independent investigation concluded that the state’s prized double agent inside the IRA’s internal security unit (codenamed Stakeknife), linked to at least 14 murders and 15 abductions—likely cost more lives than he saved. Investigators rebuked MI5 for belatedly handing over hundreds of files only after assurances that officers wouldn’t be prosecuted, while the government continues to hide behind its “neither confirm nor deny” policy to block officially naming him.

  • The Manchester Arena Inquiry: Official findings confirmed MI5 missed crucial opportunities to prevent the 2017 bombing that killed 22 people—and gave inaccurate evidence to the inquiry about the intelligence it held on bomber Salman Abedi prior to the attack.

  • Surging Agent-Handling Errors (October 2026): Just days before the high court let MI5 walk, Investigatory powers commissioner lord Leveson’s annual report revealed that MI5’s recorded agent-handling compliance errors had risen more than fivefold—from 4 in 2017 to 21 in 2025—prompting Leveson to warn bluntly that in future, MI5 “will need proof to be believed” by judges.

Act III: Meanwhile, in the Backrooms of Westminster

Intelligence chiefs argue—not without reason—that unfettered disclosure rules could chill foreign intelligence-sharing partnerships and put undercover human sources at lethal risk. Yet against a backdrop of courtroom lies, withheld files, and fatal intelligence failures, leaked internal emails published by The Grayzone suggest MI5’s leadership went far beyond legitimate national security caveats: they actively lobbied to ensure spies can never be held criminally liable for lying to an inquiry.

Following the Hillsborough disaster—where 97 Liverpool fans died and police falsified evidence to smear innocent victims—families fought for decades for a statutory duty of candour carrying 2 to 14 years in prison for officials who lie to public investigations.

In internal messages, director general sir Ken McCallum privately admitted the Hillsborough Law was “a [very] hard bill to oppose politically” because “it looks like one is against telling the truth.”

Yet oppose it they did. Using a private, pseudonymous email address, McCallum updated his predecessor, baroness Eliza Manningham-Buller—who sits as chair of the House of lords Conduct Committee—on efforts to carve out an exemption or sink the legislation outright. Together with fellow former MI5 chiefs lords Jonathan Evans and Andrew Parker, they compiled a list of friendly peers in the upper chamber, preparing briefings to warn that forcing spies to tell the truth would harm national security and spook foreign allies.

Act IV: What Must Happen Next

For decades, British establishment mythology has sold a tidy story: that its intelligence services operate under strict oversight, and that its courts stand as fearless guardians of the truth against executive overreach. November 2026 tore the mask off both.

These concrete tests will now show whether British accountability exists outside of press releases:

  1. No Blanket Carve-Outs in the lords: As the Hillsborough Law moves through its Report stage in the House of lords, peers must reject any attempt by former intelligence chiefs to exempt MI5, MI6, or GCHQ from the statutory duty of candour.

  2. A Transparent CPS Decision: The Home Office and crown Prosecution Service must publicly confirm whether Officer 2, Officer 3, and Deputy Director Witness A are under active criminal investigation for misconduct in public office—and publish full legal reasons if they are not.

  3. End the NCND Shield for Proven Lies: Courts and inquiries must enforce lord Leveson’s warning—treating MI5’s “neither confirm nor deny” claims with hard scepticism rather than automatic deference.
