Amnesty International UK’s May 2026 report Like a Snowball: the Growth and Impact of the Gender Critical Movement in the UK, expanded in the July 2026 A Growing Threat: the anti-rights movement in the UK, includes Beira’s Place (J.K. Rowling’s women-only sexual assault support centre) and For Women Scotland in its mapping of an “anti-rights ecosystem.” The briefing was temporarily removed for internal review after criticism.

The Targeted Organizations

Beira’s Place: Provides trauma-informed support exclusively for female survivors of sexual violence. The single-sex model addresses specific needs for privacy, dignity, and safety for biological women.

For Women Scotland: A volunteer-led not-for-profit company (SC669393) with no public funding and no party political alignment. Founded in 2018 in response to proposed self-ID reforms to the Gender Recognition Act. Activities include parliamentary evidence, the #sixwords campaign (securing rights for rape victims to request same-sex medical examiners), and judicial review work affirming biological sex in law.

These groups advocate for sex-based rights and protections for biological women and girls. The reports treat this advocacy as restricting rights for LGBT+ people, particularly trans women (biological males).

Key Elements and Limitations of the Reports

Media Analysis: The documents note approximately 17,000 articles on trans-related issues across selected outlets (averaging around 9 per day from 2020 to early 2025), describing the volume as disproportionate. Coverage reflected legislative proposals on self-ID, institutional policy shifts, and public concerns on safeguarding, sports, prisons, and services. The reports emphasize potential harm from the tone while giving less attention to the substantive policy debates.

Mapping and Financial Review: 117 organizations are mapped, with 51 classified as gender-critical (over half informal). Financial tracking covers 37 entities with available accounts (£144 million expenditure from 2019 to 2024, a 47% increase). The reports acknowledge blurred categories, incomplete data for many groups, and that figures represent a minimum estimate. Gender-critical spending is modest but noted for impact through litigation.

The “Anti-Rights” Label as a Rhetorical Weapon: The reports define “anti-rights actors” extremely broadly — formal and informal groups, individuals, private and state actors whose aim is to “restrict human rights by undermining human rights protections in law and practice.”

In practice, this catch-all label functions as a universal rhetorical weapon: it is applied to essentially any group or person that opposes Amnesty’s specific ideology and agenda on gender issues. By defining disagreement with their interpretation of rights (particularly defense of biological sex-based rights for women, single-sex spaces, and safeguarding) as “anti-rights,” the reports delegitimize dissent rather than engage with evidence or democratic debate. This approach turns legitimate advocacy for material reality and women’s protections into an automatic moral failing. It serves as a tool to shut down opposition instead of addressing substantive concerns raised by the public, courts, and women’s groups.

The reports link gender-critical positions to opposition to “gender ideology” and recommend regulatory scrutiny by the Charity Commission and NHS.

Regulatory Recommendations and the Push for State Power

The reports call for the Charity Commission to review charitable status for organizations seen as advocating the removal of human rights protections or targeting minority groups, and to strengthen the public benefit test. They also recommend the NHS investigate crisis pregnancy centres and avoid signposting patients to them.

In the current political climate — including the Crime and Policing Act 2026 and updated statutory Keeping Children Safe in Education (KCSIE) guidance — these calls for “regulatory scrutiny” can be seen as an attempt to utilize state apparatuses to silence specific forms of advocacy. This itself raises concerns about anti-democratic tactics: using institutional power to marginalize views that challenge the approved ideology rather than allowing open debate.

Institutional Context and Networks

Amnesty’s positions on gender frameworks align with progressive policy priorities. It has received grants from the Open Society Foundations (OSF), a major philanthropic network founded by George Soros advancing liberal global agendas. Similar patterns appear in funding for aligned organizations, such as Stonewall’s grants from the US-linked Global Equality Fund.

Leadership connections include participation in World Economic Forum activities and prior roles in media development organizations. These networks illustrate alignment across NGOs, global forums, and media institutions.

The UK Supreme Court’s 2025 ruling clarified that “woman” and “sex” in the Equality Act 2010 refer to biological sex.

Assessment and the Transparency Pivot

The reports raise points about funding transparency and organizational activities. However, their broad application of the “anti-rights” label to groups defending biological sex-based rights for women, combined with acknowledged methodological constraints, limits their analytical strength.

Advocacy for single-sex services and legal clarity on sex engages with material realities and court findings. Framing this as a primary threat to rights for trans people reflects a specific interpretive lens rather than neutral analysis.

If Amnesty demands transparency and scrutiny from the groups it labels as “anti-rights,” the same standard must apply to Amnesty and aligned networks. Full disclosure on funding flows, data sources for informal groups, leadership connections, and the practical impact of regulatory proposals is essential. Public debate benefits from evidence, legal outcomes, and space for differing perspectives.
