In the days after Microsoft’s cloud backbone collapsed on 12 November 2026—and as cascading federal breaches hit the FBI’s National Crime Information Center and the U.S. Department of Agriculture on 19–20 November—a familiar product category surged into view: email and cloud continuity. These are the paid wrappers that sit in front of Microsoft 365 and promise to keep mail flowing when the primary cloud fails. Within hours of the outage, the major vendors were already marketing the solution.

The deeper pattern is more revealing than the marketing. The three dominant players in this market are not independent security firms. They are owned by some of the largest private-equity groups in the world.

The Ownership Lair

  • Mimecast was taken private in May 2022 by Permira in a $5.8 billion deal. Permira is a formal partner of the World Economic Forum.

  • Proofpoint was acquired in 2021 by Thoma Bravo (World Economic Forum partner) in an all-cash $12.3 billion transaction. Proofpoint agreed to buy data-security specialist Varonis Systems for approximately $6.3 billion in cash—expanding its footprint from email continuity into AI-agent and corporate data-access gating.

  • Barracuda Networks was first bought by Thoma Bravo and then sold to KKR (World Economic Forum partner) in a $4 billion deal (closed in August 2022).

Just three days before the Microsoft blackout, on 9 November 2026, UK group accounts filed at Companies House revealed that Thoma Bravo extracted $840 million (£627 million) from Cambridge cybersecurity firm Darktrace—structured as a $459 million dividend plus $381 million in share buybacks—funded largely by piling $750 million in new debt arranged by Goldman Sachs onto the company’s balance sheet even as annual revenue growth slowed to 12.4% ($922.8 million). That debt-recap playbook explains why PE-backed security vendors have every financial incentive to lock public-sector buyers into rigid, recurring subscription renewals rather than resilient, self-hosted architectures.

Immediate Commercial Response

Within twenty-four hours of the Microsoft collapse, Mimecast published a blog post framed around the outage itself: “Microsoft 365 went down. Your customers still expected a reply.” The piece directed readers toward one-click failover and continuity gateways. At the same time, UK MSP and small-business guidance channels pointed buyers toward Barracuda (available through the G-Cloud framework) and Proofpoint Essentials. As third-party cloud continuity gateways buckled under the surge of failover traffic during the 12 November blackout, the failure of the primary platform was converted, almost in real time, into demand for secondary paid layers owned by private equity.

Public-Sector Double Payment

The pattern is clearest in the United Kingdom, where public bodies already sit on a projected £9 billion five-year Microsoft commitment (£1.9 billion in 2024–25 alone)—and then pay a second time for continuity wrappers on top of it.

Recent public-sector contract awards show how UK procurement rules now actively reinforce that concentration. When G-Cloud launched, it was designed to shatter the traditional IT oligopoly and open public contracts to independent British small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). Instead, as Open Cloud Coalition senior advisor Nicky Stewart told Computer Weekly, an absence of competition paved the way for an AWS and Microsoft duopoly—and the transition from G-Cloud 14 to the £14 billion, four-year G-Cloud 15 framework in autumn 2026 raises mandatory ISO, insurance, and Cyber Essentials supply-chain certification thresholds, requiring every supplier and subcontractor touching official data to hold valid certification.

Because Cyber Essentials is the UK National Cyber Security Centre’s own certification scheme, the state is effectively leveraging its own compliance gatekeeping rules—and the recurring audit overhead they impose across entire subcontractor chains—to filter out independent British SMEs in favour of hyperscalers and private-equity-backed incumbents, a shift suppliers warn puts paid to SME access altogether. Within that narrowed marketplace, public bodies routinely award continuity and perimeter contracts to private-equity-owned incumbents, including:

  • BBC – £750,000 email security renewal (direct award via GCA framework RM6098 / Computacenter)

  • DVLA – £292,000 (plus a separate encrypted email portal award)

  • Norse Group (Norfolk County Council’s wholly owned commercial services arm) – £456,402 for Mimecast licences (via Akhter Computers)

  • Local authorities, universities and police forces (Southern Housing, Uttlesford District Council, East Renfrewshire, East Suffolk, Leeds Trinity University, Staffordshire Police) awarding Mimecast, Proofpoint or Barracuda contracts ranging from £29,000 to £208,000

That same extraction loop extends directly into AI network-monitoring contracts procured through G-Cloud 14. Public procurement records show Darktrace—co-founded by MI5 and GCHQ cyber-defence veterans Stephen Huxter and Dave Palmer, led by former GCHQ deputy director Andrew France, advised by former MI5 Director-General Sir Jonathan Evans, and taken private by Thoma Bravo in 2024—holding active UK public-sector awards across Cantium Business Solutions (£620,000), Chesterfield Royal Hospital’s NHS subsidiary (£329,000), Trinity House (£244,000), Isle of Wight Council (£212,809), and Torus62 (£175,000 through 2029). When Thoma Bravo extracted $840 million (£627 million) from Darktrace in November 2026 via $750 million in new Goldman Sachs debt, multi-year UK public-sector subscriptions became part of the recurring revenue base servicing that leverage.

Meanwhile, Thoma Bravo’s flagship email-continuity vendor, Proofpoint, plugs into the parallel intelligence-to-venture pipeline documented in Part 1: under Thoma Bravo’s ownership, Proofpoint acquired Illusive Networks—the first cybersecurity spinout launched by Team8, the Israeli Unit 8200 venture foundry co-founded by Check Point CEO Nadav Zafrir—while placing former Unit 8200 section commander Elad Horn at the helm of its cloud-security product division. Across both sides of the Atlantic, alumni of state signals-intelligence agencies build perimeter tools that private equity rolls up and monetises through recurring public-sector subscriptions.

Legislative Compliance Loop

That recurring spend is no longer merely discretionary—it is being locked in by statute. Having completed its House of Lords third reading on 2 November 2026, the UK’s Cyber Security and Resilience (Network and Information Systems) Bill returns to the House of Commons on 24 November 2026—arriving on the floor just days after the Microsoft 365 collapse. By expanding regulatory scope to cover managed service providers, data centres, and critical suppliers—and imposing strict 24-hour initial and 72-hour full incident reporting mandates backed by regulator cost-recovery fees—the legislation ensures that public bodies and critical operators must continuously purchase third-party monitoring, logging, and failover wrappers to stay compliant.

The Structural Point

When a hyperscale platform fails because of a latent defect in its shared control plane, the institutional response is rarely to question the concentration itself. It is to purchase another layer of managed dependency from the firms best positioned to sell it—firms owned by private-equity houses whose business model depends on recurring subscription revenue extracted from exactly this kind of fragility.

The same logic appears across the wider pattern documented in The Fragility Dividend: the FAA outage converted into a $50 billion request directed toward a new set of concentrated telecom providers; the banking disruption revealed a shared rail owned by the largest banks themselves; the cold-chain failures were rapidly folded into sanctions and resilience narratives. In each case, the failure of a concentrated system generates further revenue, further contracts, and further lock-in for the circle of organisations already positioned around it.

No conspiracy is required.
