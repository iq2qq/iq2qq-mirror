If you thought British politics couldn’t get any more embarrassing, congratulations: Andy Burnham has found the basement and started digging with a ring light. Having glided unopposed onto the throne, he has officially turned the historic seat of government into a desperate, taxpayer-funded TikTok content big top. And the price tag for this masterclass in modern statecraft? A cool £25 million of your money, spent on influencers and social media ads this year after the government’s digital outreach unit nearly doubled its paid campaign budget. Nothing screams serious, grown-up government quite like blowing millions to try and trick Gen Z into liking you—only for the entire operation to implode in a blaze of profanity, limp DJ sets, and Whitehall misconduct scandals.

DJ Andy and the “Pussywagon” Diplomacy

Earlier this month, Downing Street opened its doors to around 60 content creators for an official reception hosted by Burnham, leaving commentators warning that Number 10 is being demeaned by influencers. Because nothing connects with struggling young voters quite like watching the Prime Minister behind a pair of DJ decks while influencers record clips on government phones—picking up right where his predecessor Keir Starmer left off when he tried throwing limp jabs at a punching bag.

Naturally, inviting 60 clout-chasers into the corridors of power went about as well as expected. TikToker Max Balegde—who was even given the floor to deliver a speech at the event—decided the iconic door of Number 10 was the ideal backdrop to mime along to a viral rave track featuring the lyrics “52 bomboclaat pussywagon chicken nuggets”. When the inevitable backlash hit, Downing Street scrambled to call the video “clearly not acceptable”. Yet in true clown-show fashion, Number 10 doesn’t rule out inviting him back to future events—and couldn’t confirm whether taxpayers had previously funded his collaborations. Meanwhile, Balegde deleted the clip, issued a half-hearted apology, and immediately pivoted to attacking “revolting right-wing people” and Kemi Badenoch for noticing what he posted.

It is the ultimate political two-step: invite influencers in to harvest young eyeballs, pretend to be shocked when they act like influencers, and let them do your partisan dirty work attacking the opposition online while Number 10 keeps its mitts clean.

Labour’s Own Rulebook vs. Number 10’s TikTok Circus

What makes this whole circus even harder to stomach is the breathtaking hypocrisy. This is a government that spends endless hours lecturing the public about cracking down on online misogyny, toxic influencers, and racial stereotyping.

Just look at Labour’s own official Code of Conduct on Afrophobia and Anti-Black Racism. It explicitly condemns "racist language, sentiments, stereotypes or actions" toward "Black Caribbean ethnic origins" and even warns members against "misogynoir"—where race and misogyny intersect. Yet when a TikTok influencer stands outside the front door of Number 10 lip-syncing Jamaican patois (bomboclaat) for cheap internet laughs, Downing Street suddenly forgets its own rulebook and calls it youth outreach.

Worse still, "bomboclaat" is literally a coarse Jamaican profanity for a menstrual cloth—meaning Number 10's star guest managed to combine Caribbean cultural stereotyping with a crude period slur right before moving on to "pussywagon" and "serving cunt". Had an ordinary party member or local councillor pulled that stunt, they’d be hauled before a disciplinary panel.

It’s the exact same story with misogyny. Earlier this year, the government launched a taxpayer-funded campaign literally headlined "Is your child influenced by toxic content?"—citing research on TikTok algorithms pushing a four-fold surge in misogynistic content—and issued school guidance to stamp out online misogyny. Meanwhile, Women and Equalities Minister Bridget Phillipson went on air to lament the "cesspit of social media" and warned just weeks ago that misogyny was being normalised "on our screens".

Fast-forward to November, and Phillipson is being wheeled out to call a Number 10 guest’s “serving c---” post “totally unacceptable”—yet Downing Street doesn’t rule him out from coming back. Even Burnham’s own allies claimed he was brought in to clean up the Starmer-era boys’ club culture of sexist briefings. Apparently, the rules on respectful language and workplace standards only apply to the plebs, not to the influencers invited through Number 10’s door.

Meanwhile, in the Throne Room...

While the front-of-house circus was busy chasing viral clout, the leadership behind the scenes was busy setting its own dumpster fire.

Ed Bearryman—the senior civil servant who founded the New Media Unit and was promoted to interim director of the Government Media Unit—recently had to step aside for “serious misconduct”. Why? Because a Cabinet Office investigation upheld allegations that he sent unwanted, inappropriate messages to a colleague 15 years his junior after a brief relationship.

And as leaked WhatsApp messages circulating on X show, the reality is every bit as toe-curling as you’d expect—from telling his younger subordinate “I do think we understand each other on a cosmic level” to reminding her “I am the boss” before asking “why don’t you say ‘lets go for a coffee tomorrow and have a hug’” when she tried to distance herself. Worse still: while the investigation was underway, Whitehall genius dictated that Bearryman should remain in charge of the unit—with the junior colleague still reporting directly to him—before rewarding him with the interim director job anyway.

Desperation Dressed Up as Outreach

Ultimately, the fact that Number 10 is compelled to play the influencer card tells you everything you need to know about how deprived this government really is. Governments with actual vision, competence, and popular support don’t need to court plastic internet personalities for relevance or throw £25 million at social media ads to manufacture applause.

When a leadership team has to rely on TikTokers to sell its message, it isn’t connecting with the next generation—it’s admitting it has nothing real left to offer. Young creators get exploited as disposable political billboards, taxpayers foot the bill, and the clowns running the circus just keep spinning the decks.
